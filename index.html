01

One-Dimensional Kinematics

Key formulas memorize

$$v_f = v_o + at$$
$$\Delta x = v_o t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$
$$\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_f+v_o)t$$
$$v_f^2 = v_o^2 + 2a\Delta x$$
$$\Delta x = v_f t - \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$
$$g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\ [D]$$

These are the "big five" constant-acceleration equations. Each one is missing exactly one of the five variables (\(v_o, v_f, a, t, \Delta x\)) — pick the equation that's missing the variable you don't know and don't need.

How to recognize this problem type understand

Motion is confined to one line (a car on a road, a ball thrown straight up/down, a cart on a ramp) and the acceleration is constant throughout the interval you're analyzing. If a problem changes acceleration partway through (like a car that speeds up then stops accelerating), you must split it into separate constant-acceleration segments.

Step-by-step strategy technique

  1. Pick a positive direction once, and stick with it. Assign a sign to every given vector quantity (velocity, acceleration, displacement).
  2. List the five variables \(v_o, v_f, a, t, \Delta x\). Fill in the ones you know; mark the one you want; identify the one you don't know and don't need.
  3. Choose the single equation missing that unneeded variable.
  4. Solve algebraically before plugging in numbers when possible — it catches sign errors.
  5. Check the sign and reasonableness of your answer against the physical picture (e.g., a decelerating car shouldn't have a negative time).

Common mistakes

  • Average speed ≠ average velocity whenever the object reverses direction. Average velocity uses net displacement; average speed uses total distance traveled. If the object reverses, you must split the motion at the instant \(v=0\) and add up the distance from each leg separately.
  • Forgetting that \(g\) always points down — if "up" is your positive direction, \(a=-9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) the entire flight, even while the object is still rising.
  • Using \(\Delta x=\tfrac12(v_f+v_o)t\) when acceleration is not constant — this equation is only valid for constant acceleration.
  • Mixing sign conventions mid-problem (e.g. calling "right" positive in one line and "left" positive two lines later).

WORKED EXAMPLE — direction reversal (multi-part, from your SchoolLoop practice test)

A car changes velocity from an initial value of 12.0 m/s to the right to a final value of 5.00 m/s to the left. The net displacement of the car during this motion is 25.0 m [R]. Find (a) the time, (b) the acceleration, (c) the average velocity, (d) the average speed.

▶ Show full solution

Recognize: constant acceleration, but the velocity changes sign — the car must slow down, stop, then speed up going backward. That's the signal average speed ≠ average velocity here.

Setup: right = positive. $$v_o=+12.0\text{ m/s},\quad v_f=-5.00\text{ m/s},\quad \Delta x = +25.0\text{ m}$$

(a) Neither \(a\) nor \(t\) is known, but the equation missing \(a\) is $$\Delta x=\tfrac12(v_f+v_o)t \Rightarrow t=\frac{2\Delta x}{v_f+v_o}=\frac{2(25.0)}{12.0+(-5.00)}=\frac{50.0}{7.00}=7.14\text{ s}$$

(b) Now use $$a=\frac{v_f-v_o}{t}=\frac{-5.00-12.0}{7.14}=-2.38\text{ m/s}^2 \Rightarrow 2.38\text{ m/s}^2\text{ [L]}$$

(c) Average velocity is just net displacement over time: $$\bar v = \frac{\Delta x}{t}=\frac{25.0}{7.14}=3.50\text{ m/s [R]}$$

(d) For average speed we need total distance. The car momentarily stops (\(v=0\)) before reversing. Distance while slowing from 12.0 to 0: $$x_1=\frac{v_o^2}{2|a|}=\frac{12.0^2}{2(2.38)}=30.25\text{ m [R]}$$ Distance while speeding up from 0 to 5.00 m/s [L]: $$x_2=\frac{v_f^2}{2|a|}=\frac{5.00^2}{2(2.38)}=5.25\text{ m [L]}$$ Total distance = 30.25 + 5.25 = 35.5 m, so $$\text{avg speed}=\frac{35.5}{7.14}=4.97\text{ m/s}$$

t = 7.14 s, a = 2.38 m/s² [L], v̄ = 3.50 m/s [R], avg speed = 4.97 m/s

Why the answer makes sense: avg speed (4.97) is bigger than the magnitude of avg velocity (3.50) — exactly what must happen whenever a trip includes backtracking, since distance ≥ |displacement| always.

Common mistake avoided: a student who computes avg speed as |Δx|/t = 3.50 m/s has silently assumed the car never reversed direction.

WORKED EXAMPLE — vertical free fall from a cliff

A boy on a cliff throws a rock straight up at \(t=0\) with initial speed 10.0 m/s. Find the rock's speed and the time when it is 45.0 m below its starting point.

▶ Show full solution

Setup: up = positive. $$v_o=+10.0\text{ m/s},\ a=-9.80\text{ m/s}^2,\ \Delta y = -45.0\text{ m (below start)}$$

Speed first (don't need \(t\)): $$v_f^2=v_o^2+2a\Delta y = (10.0)^2+2(-9.80)(-45.0)=100+882=982$$ $$v_f=\pm31.3\text{ m/s}$$ Take the negative root since the rock is moving downward when it's below the start: \(v_f = −31.3 m/s\), speed = 31.3 m/s.

Time: $$v_f=v_o+at \Rightarrow t=\frac{v_f-v_o}{a}=\frac{-31.3-10.0}{-9.80}=4.22\text{ s}$$

speed = 31.3 m/s, t = 4.22 s

Common mistake: taking the positive square root for \(v_f\). The ± from squaring always needs a physical argument (direction of travel) to pick the right sign.

BASIC

A car starts at rest and accelerates at 3.00 m/s² for 6.00 s. Find its final velocity and displacement.

Answer

v_f = 18.0 m/s; Δx = ½(3.00)(6.00)² = 54.0 m.

MEDIUM

A train decelerates from 30.0 m/s to 10.0 m/s over 200 m. Find its acceleration and the time taken.

Answer

a = (10.0²−30.0²)/(2·200) = −2.00 m/s²; t = (10.0−30.0)/(−2.00) = 10.0 s.

CHALLENGING

A ball is dropped from a height and, in the last second before hitting the ground, falls 45% of the total height. Find the total height. (Hint: let total time be T; set up the height fallen in time T−1 vs T.)

Answer

H=½gT². Height fallen by T−1 is ½g(T−1)². Setting H−½g(T−1)²=0.45H gives 0.55 = 1−(T−1)²/T² → (T−1)/T=√0.55=0.7416 → T=1/(1−0.7416)=3.87 s → H=½(9.80)(3.87)²≈73.4 m.

02

Two-Dimensional (Projectile) Motion

Key idea memorize

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent and share only one variable: time.

Horizontal (a=0): $$v_x = v_{ox}\ \ (\text{constant})$$ $$x = v_{ox}t$$
Vertical (a=−g): $$v_{fy}=v_{oy}-gt$$ $$y=v_{oy}t-\tfrac12 gt^2$$ $$v_{fy}^2=v_{oy}^2-2g\,y$$

Decompose the launch velocity: $$v_{ox}=v_o\cos\theta,\qquad v_{oy}=v_o\sin\theta$$ where θ is measured from the horizontal. Speed at any instant: $$v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$$

How to recognize it understand

Any object launched into the air with a velocity that has both a horizontal and vertical component (or purely horizontal off a height), moving only under gravity (no air resistance). Cannons, footballs, thrown balls, arrows, bundles dropped from planes.

Step-by-step strategy technique

  1. Sketch axes. Pick up/right (or a convenient compass direction) as positive.
  2. Decompose the initial velocity into \(v_{ox}\) and \(v_{oy}\).
  3. Write the horizontal and vertical equations separately, both in terms of the same \(t\).
  4. Use whichever piece of given information (a landing height, a landing angle, a specific time) to solve for \(t\) first — almost every projectile problem reduces to "find t, then everything else follows."
  5. Recombine components (Pythagorean theorem + inverse tangent) only at the very end, when the question asks for a resultant speed or direction.

Common mistakes & shortcuts

  • Never use a horizontal-motion equation with \(g\) in it, or a vertical equation with constant velocity — the two axes never mix inside one equation.
  • At the top of the trajectory, \(v_y = 0\) but \(v_x\) is not zero — this is also where the speed is at its minimum (shortcut for "minimum speed of flight" questions, provided the object doesn't hit the ground before reaching the top).
  • Landing at 45° with equal-magnitude components is a common trick: it tells you \(|v_x| = |v_{fy}|\) at impact, giving you a second equation instantly.
  • Symmetric range shortcut: a projectile launched and landing at the same height at angle θ has the same range as one launched at \((90^\circ-\theta)\), since \(\sin(2\theta)=\sin(2(90^\circ-\theta))\). Only valid for equal launch/landing height.
  • Increasing the launch angle above 45° (same speed, same height) decreases range but increases flight time — don't assume "steeper is always farther."
  • Two objects dropped/launched from the same height with the same initial vertical velocity (even if one also moves horizontally) hit the ground at the same time — horizontal motion never affects fall time.

WORKED EXAMPLE — launch from a roof, two-part (from your test on Ch. 3 & 6)

A quarterback on a roof throws a pass at \(t=0\) with initial velocity 20.0 m/s [S30°U] (30° above horizontal, horizontal part pointing south). (a) Find the speed at t = 2.5 s. (b) The ball lands at 45°, so the horizontal and vertical speed components are equal in magnitude at impact. At what height y above the ground did the ball leave his hand?

▶ Show full solution

Decompose: $$v_{ox}=20.0\cos30°=17.3\text{ m/s [S]}\ (\text{constant})$$ $$v_{oy}=20.0\sin30°=10.0\text{ m/s [U]}$$

(a) At t = 2.5 s: $$v_x=17.3\text{ m/s (unchanged)}$$ $$v_y=v_{oy}-gt=10.0-9.80(2.5)=-14.5\text{ m/s (14.5 m/s down)}$$ $$v=\sqrt{17.3^2+14.5^2}=\sqrt{300+210}=\sqrt{510}=22.6\text{ m/s}$$

(b) Landing at 45° with equal components means \(v_{fy}=-17.3\,\mathrm{m/s}\) (must equal \(|v_x|\) in magnitude). Find the time this occurs: $$-17.3=10.0-9.80t\Rightarrow t=\frac{10.0+17.3}{9.80}=2.79\text{ s}$$ Now find the vertical displacement over that time (this equals minus the launch height above the ground, since it starts at height y and ends at the ground): $$\Delta y=v_{oy}t-\tfrac12 gt^2=10.0(2.79)-4.90(2.79)^2=27.9-38.1=-10.2\text{ m}$$ The ball fell 10.2 m below the roof, so it left the quarterback's hand \(y = 10.2 m\) above the ground.

v(2.5s) = 22.6 m/s; y = 10.2 m

Why it makes sense: the ball is still gaining downward speed after 2.5s (it hasn't landed yet, since landing requires 2.79 s), so a speed of 22.6 m/s that's larger than the 20.0 m/s launch speed is reasonable — it has fallen further than it rose.

WORKED EXAMPLE — plateau launch, solve for range (from your Kinematics test)

A gun on top of a 200 m plateau fires a projectile at 150 m/s [N35°U]. It lands at the bottom of the plateau (200 m below). Find the range and the minimum speed during flight.

▶ Show full solution

$$v_{ox}=150\cos35°=122.9\text{ m/s},\quad v_{oy}=150\sin35°=86.0\text{ m/s}$$

Vertical equation with \(Δy = −200 m\): $$-200=86.0t-4.90t^2 \Rightarrow 4.90t^2-86.0t-200=0$$ Quadratic formula (take the positive root — the physically meaningful one): $$t=\frac{86.0+\sqrt{86.0^2+4(4.90)(200)}}{2(4.90)}=19.6\text{ s}$$

Range: $$x=v_{ox}t=122.9(19.6)=2.41\times10^3\text{ m}$$

Minimum speed occurs at the top of the arc, where \(v_y=0\), so speed = \(v_{ox} = 123\) m/s (only valid because the projectile does reach and pass through the top of its arc before landing, which it does here since it's launched upward).

Range = 2.41 × 10³ m; minimum speed = 123 m/s

Common mistake avoided: taking the negative root of the quadratic, which gives a negative (meaningless) time.

BASIC

A ball is launched horizontally at 12.0 m/s from a 20.0 m tall ledge. Find the time to land and the horizontal range.

Answer

t=√(2·20.0/9.80)=2.02 s; range = 12.0(2.02) = 24.2 m.

MEDIUM

A projectile is launched from level ground at 25.0 m/s at 40.0° above horizontal. Find the max height, flight time, and range.

Answer

v_oy=16.1 m/s → max height = v_oy²/2g = 13.2 m; total flight time = 2v_oy/g = 3.28 s; v_ox=19.2 m/s → range=v_ox·t=62.9 m.

CHALLENGING

A cannon fires a projectile at 60.0 m/s [R50°U]. It strikes a canyon wall 50.0 m below its launch height, 400 m horizontally away. Verify this is consistent, and find the magnitude of the total displacement at impact.

Answer

v_ox=38.6 m/s, v_oy=46.0 m/s. t=400/38.6=10.4 s. Δy=46.0(10.4)−4.90(10.4)²=478−530=−52≈−50 m ✓ (consistent). Displacement magnitude=√(400²+50²)=403 m.

03

Relative Velocity & Vector Addition

Key formula memorize

$$\vec v_{AC} = \vec v_{AB} + \vec v_{BC}$$

Read the subscripts like fractions that cancel: velocity of A relative to C = (velocity of A relative to B) + (velocity of B relative to C), where the inner "B"s cancel. Equivalently, to isolate one term, rearrange like ordinary vector algebra, e.g. $$\vec v_{BC}=\vec v_{AC}-\vec v_{AB}$$

How to recognize it understand

Two frames of reference are involved: a swimmer/boat and a moving current, a plane and wind, or two moving observers describing the same third object differently ("Person X sees Person Y moving at..."). Any time a velocity is described "relative to" something other than the ground, this is the topic.

Step-by-step strategy technique

  1. Identify the three "actors": usually an object (boat, swimmer, plane, person), a medium (water, air, another moving person), and the ground.
  2. Write the vector equation with subscripts so the middle letters cancel: \(\vec v_{\text{object,ground}} = \vec v_{\text{object,medium}} + \vec v_{\text{medium,ground}}\).
  3. Break every vector into x/y (e.g. East/North) components — this turns vector addition into simple arithmetic.
  4. Solve for the unknown vector's components, then convert back to magnitude (Pythagorean theorem) and direction (inverse tangent) only at the end.
  5. For "reach the far bank fastest" or "go straight across" river problems, remember: only the component of velocity perpendicular to the current affects crossing time; the current only causes sideways drift.

Common mistakes

  • Adding vectors by adding magnitudes directly instead of components — only valid if the vectors are parallel.
  • Confusing "swim so that you land directly across" (aim upstream, resultant speed is less than swim speed) with "swim so your resultant speed relative to shore is some value" (a different geometry entirely — solve for the resultant, not the heading, or vice versa, don't assume which is fixed).
  • Sign/direction errors: always draw a compass rose and double check whether "East" is +x and "North" is +y before decomposing.
  • Forgetting that \(\vec v_{AB} = -\vec v_{BA}\) (velocity of A relative to B is the negative of B relative to A).

WORKED EXAMPLE — two moving observers (from your test on Ch. 3 & 6)

Michael walks at 2.0 m/s [N] relative to the ground. From Julie's point of view (relative to Julie), Michael's velocity is 2.1 m/s [E45°N]. Find Julie's speed relative to the ground.

▶ Show full solution

Set up the vector equation. Let M = Michael, J = Julie, G = ground: $$\vec v_{MG}=\vec v_{MJ}+\vec v_{JG} \ \Rightarrow\ \vec v_{JG}=\vec v_{MG}-\vec v_{MJ}$$

Take East = +x, North = +y: $$\vec v_{MG}=(0,\ 2.0)$$ $$\vec v_{MJ}=(2.1\cos45°,\ 2.1\sin45°)=(1.48,\ 1.48)$$

Subtract: $$\vec v_{JG}=(0-1.48,\ 2.0-1.48)=(-1.48,\ 0.52)$$

Magnitude: $$|\vec v_{JG}|=\sqrt{(-1.48)^2+(0.52)^2}=\sqrt{2.19+0.27}=\sqrt{2.46}=1.57\text{ m/s}$$

Julie's speed relative to ground = 1.57 m/s (direction [W19°N])

Why it makes sense: Julie must be moving generally westward relative to the ground, because Michael (who is walking north relative to the ground) appears to Julie to be moving east-of-north — Julie's own motion has to be westward to produce that apparent shift.

Common mistake: writing \(\vec v_{MG}=\vec v_{JG}+\vec v_{MJ}\) with the wrong sign — always isolate algebraically from the subscript-cancellation equation rather than guessing which vector adds or subtracts.

WORKED EXAMPLE — river crossing (from your worksheet)

Michael Phelps can swim at 2.0 m/s in still water. He swims across a river that flows south at 1.5 m/s and is 2.0 km wide, starting on the west bank. In what direction (relative to the water) must he swim to move straight east, and how long does the crossing take?

▶ Show full solution

Key idea: to end up moving straight east relative to the ground, his velocity relative to the water must have a northward component that exactly cancels the southward current — the current is 1.5 m/s [S], so he needs +1.5 m/s [N] built into his swimming direction.

His swim speed relative to water is fixed at 2.0 m/s, so: $$v_{\text{north component}}=1.5\text{ m/s}\Rightarrow \sin\phi=\frac{1.5}{2.0}\Rightarrow \phi = 48.6°$$ measured from east toward north: heading [E49°N].

East component (this becomes his full ground speed, since north components cancel): $$v_{\text{east}}=\sqrt{2.0^2-1.5^2}=\sqrt{1.75}=1.32\text{ m/s}$$

Crossing time uses only the component that actually gets him across (east), and only the crossing distance (2.0 km = 2000 m): $$t=\frac{2000}{1.32}=1.52\times10^3\text{ s} \approx 25\text{ min}$$

Heading [E49°N]; t = 1.52 × 10³ s ≈ 25 min

Common mistake: using the full 2.0 m/s swim speed to compute crossing time — only the component of velocity perpendicular to the bank (east, here) contributes to crossing; the north component is "wasted" canceling the current.

Practice: basic → challenging

BASIC

A plane flies at 200 m/s [E] relative to the air. The wind blows at 40 m/s [N]. Find the plane's velocity relative to the ground.

Answer

v=√(200²+40²)=204 m/s, direction [E11°N] (atan(40/200)=11.3°).

MEDIUM

A boat that can go 4.0 m/s in still water crosses a 100 m wide river with a 3.0 m/s current, aiming straight across (not compensating). Find the resultant speed and the drift downstream.

Answer

resultant speed=√(4²+3²)=5.0 m/s; crossing time=100/4.0=25 s; drift=3.0(25)=75 m downstream.

CHALLENGING

To a driver heading [N], a passing train appears to move at 30 m/s [E20°N]. If the train's true (ground) velocity is 25 m/s [E], find the driver's (car's) speed.

Answer

\(\vec v_{\text{train,car}}=(30\cos20°,30\sin20°)=(28.2,10.3)\); \(\vec v_{\text{train,ground}}=(25,0)\); \(\vec v_{\text{car,ground}}=\vec v_{\text{train,ground}}-\vec v_{\text{train,car}}=(25-28.2,\ 0-10.3)=(-3.2,-10.3)\); speed \(=\sqrt{3.2^2+10.3^2}=10.8\) m/s.

04

Circular Motion (Uniform & Combined)

Key formulas memorize

$$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}$$
$$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}$$
$$\vec a = \vec a_t + \vec a_c$$
$$a=\sqrt{a_t^2+a_c^2}$$

Centripetal acceleration \(a_c\) always points toward the center of the circular path and changes only the direction of velocity. Tangential acceleration \(a_t\) is along the direction of motion and changes only the speed. The two are always perpendicular, so their resultant magnitude comes from the Pythagorean theorem.

How to recognize it understand

Anything moving along a curved/circular path: a ball on a string, a car rounding a curve or cresting a hill, a coin on a turntable, an orbiting planet, a pendulum. If speed is changing while the object turns, both \(a_t\) and \(a_c\) exist simultaneously — this is a favorite "trick" test question (see the pendulum example below).

Step-by-step strategy technique

  1. Identify the radius of the actual circular path — for a conical/swinging pendulum tilted at angle θ from the horizontal, the radius is \(r=L\cos\theta\) (L = string length), not the string length itself.
  2. Find speed either from \(v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}\) (given a period) or from other kinematics.
  3. Compute \(a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}\), directed toward the center.
  4. If the object is also speeding up/slowing down along the path, compute \(a_t\) separately (ordinary 1-D kinematics along the direction of motion).
  5. Combine perpendicular components with the Pythagorean theorem for total acceleration; use inverse tangent for its direction relative to one of the components.

Common mistakes & the classic pendulum trap

  • "Speed = 0 means acceleration = 0" is FALSE. At the highest point of a pendulum's swing (momentarily at rest), \(a_c = v²/r = 0\), but the pendulum is about to swing back — it has nonzero tangential acceleration (gravity's component along the path). Total acceleration equals the tangential component alone at that instant, and it is not zero.
  • Doubling the radius does not halve \(a_c\) unless speed is held fixed — check whether the problem holds \(v\) or T constant, since \(a_c=v^2/r\) and \(a_c=\frac{4\pi^2r}{T^2}\) depend oppositely on r.
  • Using the string length instead of the horizontal radius for a conical pendulum's centripetal calculations.
  • Forgetting that a car cresting a hill has centripetal acceleration pointing downward (toward the center of curvature, which is below the road), so it must be combined vectorially (not just added as a number) with any forward/backward tangential acceleration.

WORKED EXAMPLE — pendulum at the top of its swing (from your test's conceptual FRQ)

A pendulum swings between points A and C, momentarily at rest at each. A student claims: "the speed at point C is zero, and since a_c = v²/r, the acceleration must be zero there." Explain what's wrong.

▶ Show full solution

What's right: it's true that \(a_c = v²/r = 0\) at point C, since v = 0 there.

What's missing: total acceleration is the vector sum \(\vec a=\vec a_t+\vec a_c\), not \(a_c\) alone. At point C the pendulum is about to swing back down toward B — gravity still has a component along the direction of motion (tangent to the arc), producing a nonzero \(a_t\) pointing generally toward B.

Since \(a_c=0\) at that instant, the total acceleration equals the tangential piece alone: $$\vec a = \vec a_t \neq 0$$

Why this matters generally: "zero velocity" only forces "zero centripetal acceleration" — it never implies zero total acceleration, because tangential acceleration depends on the net tangential force (here, gravity), not on speed.

WORKED EXAMPLE — car cresting a hill, combined a_t and a_c (from your worksheet)

A car crests a hill at 20.0 m/s with a forward (tangential) acceleration of 1.0 m/s². The radius of curvature of the hill at that point is 100 m. Find the car's net acceleration.

▶ Show full solution

Centripetal acceleration (points downward, toward the center of the hill's curvature): $$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\frac{20.0^2}{100}=4.00\text{ m/s}^2$$

Tangential acceleration (horizontal, forward): \(a_t = 1.0 m/s²\). These are perpendicular, so: $$a=\sqrt{a_t^2+a_c^2}=\sqrt{1.0^2+4.00^2}=\sqrt{17.0}=4.12\text{ m/s}^2$$ Direction below the forward horizontal: $$\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{4.00}{1.0}\right)=76°$$

a = 4.12 m/s², directed [forward 76° down]

Common mistake avoided: simply adding 1.0 + 4.00 = 5.0 m/s² — that's only valid if the two accelerations were parallel; here they're perpendicular, so the Pythagorean theorem applies instead.

WORKED EXAMPLE — conical pendulum (from your worksheet)

A 0.85 m string swings a ball in a horizontal circle with period 1.3 s. The string makes 30° with the horizontal. Find the ball's speed and acceleration.

▶ Show full solution

Key step: the radius of the circular path is the horizontal projection of the string, not the string length itself: $$r = L\cos30° = 0.85(0.866)=0.736\text{ m}$$

Speed: $$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}=\frac{2\pi(0.736)}{1.3}=3.56\text{ m/s}$$

Acceleration (purely centripetal here — constant speed, uniform circular motion): $$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\frac{3.56^2}{0.736}=17.2\text{ m/s}^2$$

v = 3.56 m/s; a = 17.2 m/s² (toward the axis)

Practice: basic → challenging

BASIC

A coin sits 15 cm from the center of a turntable spinning once per second. Find its speed and centripetal acceleration.

Answer

v=2π(0.15)/1.0=0.942 m/s; a_c=0.942²/0.15=5.92 m/s².

MEDIUM

A planet orbits its star with period 4.32×10⁶ s and orbital radius 1.10×10¹⁰ m. Find its centripetal acceleration.

Answer

a_c=4π²r/T²=4π²(1.10×10¹⁰)/(4.32×10⁶)²=2.33×10⁻² m/s², toward the star.

CHALLENGING

A car goes around a curve of radius R at speed v. It then goes around a curve of radius 2R at speed 2v. Compare the centripetal accelerations (ratio, second to first).

Answer

a_c1=v²/R. a_c2=(2v)²/(2R)=4v²/2R=2v²/R=2·a_c1. The second acceleration is twice as big.

Test-Day Checklist

  • Pick a positive direction before writing any numbers, and don't change it mid-problem.
  • Write out all five 1-D kinematics variables and circle the one you don't know and don't need.
  • For projectile motion, decompose immediately — never mix horizontal and vertical quantities in one equation.
  • For relative velocity, write the full subscript-cancellation vector equation before touching components.
  • For circular motion, ask: is speed constant? If not, you need both \(a_t\) and \(a_c\), combined with the Pythagorean theorem — never just added.
  • Watch for the "v = 0 ⇒ a = 0" trap — it's false whenever tangential acceleration is present.
  • Use three significant figures and scientific notation for numbers >100 or <0.10, and always give magnitude and direction for vector answers.
  • Sanity-check every answer: is the sign right? Is the size physically reasonable?
